# Ruanruan and the Matches

It was the last day of the year. It was bitterly cold, the snow would not stop, and it was nearly dark.

Ruanruan walked along the street with nothing on her head and nothing on her feet. She had had shoes when she left home: a pair of old slippers far too big for her, which had been her mother's. Crossing the road, two carriages came hurtling at her and she scrambled out of the way, and one slipper was gone. The other had barely landed in the snow when a little grey-and-white husky shot out of an alley, snatched it up in his mouth and ran. At the corner Haide looked back at her, showed the tip of one small fang, and was gone.

<!-- illustration:1|The stolen slipper -->

She didn't call out. She never called out loud.

Her old, worn pink apron was full of matches, and she held one small box in her hand. All day nobody had bought a single one, and nobody had given her a single coin. Cold and hungry, she shivered as she walked. Snowflakes settled in her coral-pink hair, and the two low pigtails drooped lower under the weight. She was a pitiful sight.

Every window was lit, and the street smelled of roast goose. It was New Year's Eve.

In a corner between two houses she sat down and curled up, tucking her little feet under her, but she only grew colder. She didn't dare go home. She hadn't sold one match or earned one coin, and her father would beat her. Besides, it was just as cold at home; the cracks in the roof were stuffed with straw and rags, and the wind still got in.

Her hands were almost numb. Perhaps one match would warm her a little?

She drew one out and struck it on the wall.

*Scritch.* It flared. The little flame was warm and bright, and she cupped her hands round it like a tiny candle. It seemed to her she was sitting in front of a great iron stove with shining brass feet and handles, the fire burning so high and so warm. She had just stretched out her feet to warm them—

The flame went out. The stove was gone. All she held was a burnt stub.

She struck another.

Where the light fell, the wall turned thin as gauze, and she could see right into the room. On a snow-white tablecloth stood fine china, and on a dish a roast goose steamed, stuffed with apples and plums. And then, most wonderful of all, the goose hopped down off the dish, a knife and fork still stuck in its back, and came waddling across the floor towards her—

The match went out. In front of her was only the thick, cold wall.

She struck a third.

This time she was sitting under an enormous Christmas tree, bigger and lovelier than the one she had seen last year through the glass at a rich merchant's house. Thousands of candles burned on its green branches, and coloured pictures like the ones in the shop windows looked down at her. She reached up towards them—

The match went out. The thousands of candle flames rose higher and higher until they were the stars in the sky. One of them fell, drawing a long streak of fire across the dark.

"Someone is dying," Ruanruan said softly.

Her grandmother had told her that. When a star falls, she had said, a soul is going up to God. Her grandmother was the only person in the world who had ever been kind to her, and she was gone.

She struck another.

In its light stood her grandmother, so gentle, so kind.

"Grandma!" Ruanruan cried out. It was the loudest she had ever called in her life. "Take me with you! I know you'll be gone when the match goes out, like the stove, like the goose, like the Christmas tree…"

Quickly she struck every match that was left, the whole bundle, not one held back. She wanted to keep her grandmother there.

The matches blazed brighter than daylight. Her grandmother had never been so tall or so beautiful. She took Ruanruan in her arms, and the two of them flew away in light and joy, higher and higher, to where there was no cold, no hunger and nothing to be afraid of.

In the morning they found her in the corner. Her cheeks were red, and she was smiling. She had frozen to death on the last night of the old year.

The New Year's sun shone on her small body. Burnt matches lay scattered around her; one whole bundle had burned down.

"She was trying to warm herself," people said.

Nobody knew what beautiful things she had seen, or in what brightness she had gone with her grandmother into the New Year.
